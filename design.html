Zerops Challenge · plan B · for review

The watcher that lives inside

A tool that sits inside your Zerops project, watches your other services from the inside, and when one stops answering, opens a room where people can see it, talk about it, and fix it without leaving the page.

Checked, not assumed

Plan B depended on Zerops letting your app list its own services and restart them. I pulled the live API spec and confirmed all three pieces exist. Two of them turned out better than expected — see What the API gave us below.

One

What it does, in one breath

Every five seconds it knocks on the door of each service. If a service doesn't answer twice in a row, it declares a problem, opens a room about it, and tells everyone who's looking. Inside that room you can see who else is there, type notes to each other, and press a button that restarts the broken service. When it comes back, the room closes itself. Afterwards you can rewind the whole thing and watch it again from the start.

Three

Three choices that look like mistakes

Flagging these up front so they don't read as sloppiness later.

Only one copy of probe, deliberately

The instinct is to run two so it can't die. That's wrong here. Two copies means every service gets knocked on twice, two separate brains disagreeing about what's broken, and two problem reports for one outage. One copy is correct. If it dies, Zerops starts it again, and a few seconds of blindness is a much better trade than double-reporting.

Two copies of api, also deliberately

This is the one that makes the intercom necessary instead of decorative. Your browser is connected to copy one. The problem is found by probe, which doesn't know your browser exists. Copy one only hears about it because it's listening to the intercom. Build this part first — it's the piece that quietly doesn't work if you leave it until 3am, and it's the hardest thing to bolt on afterwards.

The fake services aren't on the internet

Not laziness — the entire point. On stage you can say: "these three aren't reachable from anywhere. Try it. My tool can see them because it lives on their network." That's the whole Zerops argument made visible, and it costs nothing to set up.

Four

How it decides something is broken

This lives entirely in probe, and it's the part most likely to be subtly wrong, so it's worth being precise.

  • Every 5 seconds, ask each service for its health page. Give it 2 seconds to answer.
  • Anything that isn't a normal yes counts as a miss — too slow, refused, or an error page.
  • Two misses in a row and the service is declared down. A problem opens.
  • Two hits in a row and it's declared healthy again. The problem closes itself.

Why two, and not one or three

One miss would mean a single hiccup wakes everyone up. Three misses takes fifteen seconds, which is an eternity when someone is watching you demo. Two gives you ten seconds — slow enough to ignore noise, fast enough that nobody's attention has wandered.

Every knock gets recorded either way, so the screen can show live response times. But only a change of state opens or closes a problem. That distinction is what stops the timeline filling up with noise.

The one guard that matters

If probe restarts halfway through an outage, it wakes up with no memory and could open a second problem for something already broken. Fix it in the database, not in code: a rule that says one service can only have one open problem at a time. Then a duplicate is impossible rather than unlikely.

Five

What gets written down

Four tables. The trick is the last one: nothing is ever edited or deleted, only added. That's what makes rewinding free — you replay the list in order.

services

The things being watched. Filled in automatically by asking Zerops.

  • name, hostname, port
  • up / down / unknown
  • how many containers
  • last checked

checks

Every single knock and what came back.

  • answered? yes / no
  • how long it took
  • what went wrong

incidents

One row per outage. Open until it isn't.

  • which service
  • opened at
  • closed at (or empty)

incident_events

The story, in order. Never edited. This is what rewind reads.

  • noticed / recovered
  • someone said something
  • someone joined
  • restart asked for
  • restart finished
Six

What the API gave us

I pulled the real spec rather than guessing. Everything B needs is there, and two things came back better than hoped.

What we neededWhat exists
Find my own servicesGET /project/{id}/service-stack
Restart a servicePUT /service-stack/{id}/restart
Know when it finishedGET /process/{id}
Count containers (bonus)GET /service-stack/{id}/container

Restart doesn't finish immediately — and that's good

Asking for a restart hands you back a ticket, not an answer. You check the ticket until it says done. That sounds like extra work but it's the best thing that could have happened: the room can show restarting… as a real live state instead of a button that flashes and leaves everyone guessing.

You can count containers for free

There's an endpoint that tells you how many containers a service is running right now. That means the container count can sit on every card and go up and down on its own as Zerops scales things. It's the most eye-catching thing Zerops does, and it costs you one API call rather than a whole second project.

Seven

The moment the whole thing is built around

Everything above exists to make these eight seconds work. Someone presses Restart inside the room:

  1. The screen sends the request to api.
  2. api asks Zerops to restart that service.
  3. Zerops hands back a ticket number.
  4. A line appears in the room: “Arogya asked for a restart.” Everyone watching sees it at the same time.
  5. api quietly checks the ticket every couple of seconds.
  6. Ticket says done. Another line appears: “restart finished.”
  7. Meanwhile probe knocks and gets an answer. Then another.
  8. The problem closes itself. The card goes green. Nobody refreshed anything.

Noticed it. Talked about it. Fixed it. Confirmed the fix. All on one screen, in front of the person judging it. That's the sentence the whole project is trying to earn.

Eight

When things go wrong

If this breaksWhat happens
Zerops won't talk to us Keep using the last list of services we saved. Show a small banner saying so. Knocking carries on as normal — it never needed the API. The restart button greys out and says why.
We've never reached Zerops at all Fall back to a hand-typed list of services in the settings. Twenty minutes of work, and it means a broken token can never take the demo down.
A restart fails Write it into the room as a failure, with the reason Zerops gave. Never swallow it silently — a button that does nothing is worse than one that says it couldn't.
Someone's connection drops The browser reconnects on its own and asks for everything it missed. It catches up rather than starting blank.
probe restarts mid-outage It rebuilds what it knew from the database. The one-open-problem-per-service rule stops it double-reporting.
An api copy dies Browsers reconnect to the other one and keep streaming. Worth doing on purpose during the demo — it looks like magic and it's free.
Nine

What I'd test, and what I wouldn't

There isn't time for real coverage, so spend it only where a bug would ruin the demo.

Worth it
  • The deciding logic. Miss, miss, declared down. Hit, hit, declared healthy. Never two open problems for one service. This is where quiet bugs live.
  • One end-to-end check. probe writes something, it lands in the database, it comes out of the intercom. Proves the spine works.
  • A written demo script. The exact sequence of clicks. Run it twice before recording.
Skip
  • Tests on the screens. They change every hour and you'll see breakage instantly.
  • Load tests. Not what's being judged.
  • Anything chasing a coverage number.
Ten

The 24 hours

0 – 1 All eight services up and empty. A live URL that works. And prove the token actually lists your services — if that fails, you find out now, not at 3am.
1 – 3 The intercom. Two copies of api. Publish a fake event by hand and watch it land in two browser windows at once. Nothing else starts until this works.
3 – 6 Knocking, the deciding logic, the database. Cards go red and green for real.
6 – 8 Ask Zerops what services exist. Put container counts on the cards.
8 – 12 Rooms: opening by themselves, the timeline, typing to each other, seeing who's there.
12 – 14 The restart button. The reason you picked this plan.
14 – 17 Rewind.
17 – 19 Polish. Seed some history so it doesn't look brand new. Rig one fake service to fall over on its own.
19 – 21 Rehearse twice. Record the video.
21 – 24 Write-up, README, submission form, and slack.

If you fall behind, cut in this order

Rewind first. Then seeing who's there. Then typing to each other. Then automatic discovery — fall back to the hand-typed list, nobody watching will know.

Never cut the restart button. It's the only reason this plan was worth the risk. If it goes, you've built plan A with extra steps.

Eleven

Things I decided without asking

Say the word on any of these and I'll change it before we write the plan.

It doesn't need logins. You type a name when you join a room and that's it. Real accounts would eat three hours and add nothing a judge can see.
Updates are pushed one way, not two. The server pushes to the screen; the screen posts back normally. Much less code than a two-way pipe, reconnects on its own, and nothing here needs the extra speed.
It watches everything in the project, including itself. Self-watching is a nice touch and costs nothing. Easy to hide the tool's own services if it looks cluttered.
The name is a placeholder. Nothing is committed to it yet.
